Introduction

The movement of a boundary between two different traffic states (speed, density, or flow).

Vehicles Travel at the Same Speed

One Car Suddenly Breaks

Braking Wave moves backward

More cars slow down, queue begins the form

Cars move forward, but the effect moves backward (A long line of cars is slowing down even though the original cause may already be gone)

This wave of braking moves through the traffic is called aTRAFFIC SHOCKWAVE.

Fluid Flow Analogy

Ripple Effect

When a stone falls into a river, it transfers its kinetic energy to the water's surface, causing the surrounding water molecules to be displaced.

This disturbance creates concentric waves (ripples), that propagate outward from the point of impact.

These waves form because water is a fluid that naturally seeks to restore equilibrium after its surface is disturbed.

As the displaced water moves, forces such as surface tension and gravity act together to smooth the disturbance, producing oscillating waves that spread in all directions.

The ripple effect continues as the energy is gradually transferred through the water. Over time, the waves dissipate as their energy is lost to friction and other resistive forces, allowing the water to return to its calm, undisturbed state.

The water doesn’t move far, but the ripple travels outward

And traffic behaves similarly...

Shockwave Classification

1.Backward Shockwave

Car move forward but the traffic jam moves backward (The congestion moves toward approaching vehicles).

Example 1

This illustrates a backward-moving shockwave caused by an accident.

Vehicles behind the crash are closely packed while vehicles ahead of the crash continue moving because they have already passed the obstruction.

Example 2

This illustrates a backward-moving shockwave due to a fixed bottleneck.

A toll booth is present. Multiple vehicles are lined up waiting to pass through the toll booths.

Congestion forms upstream of the toll gate while vehicles leave one by one.

2.Forward Shockwaves

Boundary moves in the same direction as traffic and Recovery region moves forward.

Example

This illustrates a forward-moving shockwave caused by green traffic light.

It usually happens when congestion disappears. As the traffic light turns green, cars begin accelerating.

3. Stationary Shockwaves

Boundary does not move. The bottleneck arrival rate equals the departure rate.

Example

This illustrates a stationary shockwave caused by an efficient toll system.

Vehicles slow down at the bottleneck (such as a toll booth), but the queue neither grows nor shrinks.

Imagine vehicles approaching a toll plaza at 1,800 veh/hr, and the toll system can also process 1,800 veh/hr. Cars slow down to pay, then accelerate again.

The transition between free-flow traffic and slower toll traffic stays at the toll plaza, so the shockwave is stationary.

Top View of the Road at an Instant of Time

Phantom Traffic Jam

Initially, vehicles travel at uniform speed.

After sometime, vehicles are catching up with each other wherein some vehicles stop which create a shockwaves or a series of brakes or stops for the succeeding vehicles behind it even though traffic is continuously progressing.

Flow Density Curve of the Stream

The flow–density diagram illustrates how traffic transitions from one operating state to another when a slow-moving truck enters and later exits the traffic stream.

Each point on the curve represents a traffic state, while the line joining two states represents the shockwave separating them.

State 1

Slope of line 0-1 (50 kph - speed of the vehicle before entry of the truck)

State 2

Slope of line 0-2 (30 kph - speed of the vehicle after entry of the truck)

State 3

Slope of line 1-2 is the velocity of the shockwave:$ \omega = \frac{q_2-q_1}{k_2-k_1} $.

When the truck decides to exit, the vehicles will slowly accelerate and will travel at the capacity of the roadway, and slowly will reach normal speed at 50 kph.

State 2

Slope of line 1-2 (After entry of the truck)

State 3
